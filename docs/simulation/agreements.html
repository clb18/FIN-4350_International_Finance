Trade and Financial Agreements

1 When agreements become available

Beginning in Round 3 (or when the instructor announces), countries may pursue bilateral agreements to strengthen trade ties or stabilize financial conditions.

Note

Agreements are optional. You may choose to negotiate, refuse, or counteroffer. However, agreements can materially affect credibility, capital flows, and trade outcomes.

2 What agreements can and cannot do

2.1 Agreements can:

  • reduce trade frictions and improve market access
  • provide emergency liquidity support (swap lines)
  • signal credibility through commitments
  • create cooperation (or dependence) that affects outcomes

2.2 Agreements cannot:

  • override instructor-imposed shocks or constraints
  • eliminate all risk (markets still react)
  • guarantee favorable outcomes if policies are inconsistent
Important

Consistency rule: If a country signs an agreement and then violates it without a credible justification, markets will impose a credibility penalty.

3 Agreement types (Phase 1)

During the first phase (Rounds 3–6), the simulation permits two agreement types:

3.1 1. Trade Agreement (TA)

A bilateral deal focused on goods/services trade.

Typical provisions (choose 2–3 max): - tariff reductions (general or sector-specific) - removal of non-tariff barriers (quotas, licensing delays) - mutual recognition of standards or customs fast-tracking - export restriction commitments (e.g., no sudden bans)

Common trade-offs - domestic political backlash (protected industries) - competitiveness shifts across countries - distributional impacts (business vs labor)

3.2 2. Central Bank Swap Line (SL)

A bilateral liquidity backstop providing foreign currency access during stress.

Typical provisions (choose 2–3 max): - size of the line (small / medium / large tier) - trigger conditions (only during stress; discretionary; automatic) - policy conditions (e.g., maintain inflation target band; fiscal discipline) - maturity/renewal terms

Common trade-offs - moral hazard concerns (“bailout” perception) - loss of autonomy (conditions) - reputational risk for the provider

Warning

A swap line is not free money. It improves liquidity and credibility, but may increase political tension and conditionality.

4 Negotiation rules (simple and enforceable)

4.1 Proposal limits

  • Each country may submit at most one (1) proposal per round.
  • The instructor may approve at most two (2) agreements per round for activation (to keep the system manageable).

4.2 Acceptance requirement

Agreements only take effect if the partner country submits an acceptance (or both submit a shared final text).

4.3 Duration

Choose one: - 1 round - 2 rounds - Indefinite (until explicitly canceled)

4.4 Cancellation and violation

  • Countries may cancel an agreement by announcing cancellation one round in advance.
  • Violations without credible justification lead to:
    • credibility penalty
    • higher risk premium / reduced capital inflows
    • reduced willingness of partners to negotiate

5 What to submit (Canvas)

Each agreement submission includes: 1. Proposal Form (one page) 2. Partner Response (accept / reject / counter) 3. Optional: Short Memo (≤ 1 page) if the instructor requests it for grading

See also: Decisions and Rules and Weekly Briefs

11 How agreements affect outcomes (market logic)

Agreements influence outcomes through: - Credibility: commitment signals reduce perceived risk - Trade volumes: lower frictions expand trade over time - Capital flows: swap lines and credible cooperation reduce “sudden stop” risk - Spillovers: partners’ conditions transmit across countries

Note

Instructor note (how you’ll see results): Outcomes will be reported directionally (↑ ↓ stable) with short causal explanations linking decisions to market reactions.

12 Grading (if used)

If agreements are graded in a given round, evaluation focuses on: - Economic logic (does the deal address the stated problem?) - Coherence (terms are consistent with your country’s framework) - Trade-offs (acknowledges winners/losers domestically) - Feasibility (realistic commitments and enforcement)

13 Phase 2 (post-shock expansion)

After the shock and realignment (Act II), the instructor may allow additional agreement types, such as: - regional trade blocs - reserve pooling arrangements - currency bands/basket pegs - investment treaties (FDI protections)

These will be announced at the time they become available.
